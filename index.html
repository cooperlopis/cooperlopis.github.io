NorCal40A CW Transceiver

RF & analog ~3 months 13 board modules 40 m · 7 MHz

Overview

The NorCal40A is a 40-meter QRP CW transceiver. I built mine alongside David Rutledge's Electronics of Radio, which structures the project as thirteen separate modules — mixer, VFO, IF amplifier, product detector, transmit chain — each with its own theory section and its own set of measurements before moving on.

Total build time was roughly three months. Rather than populating the whole board at once, I brought up the receiver chain and transmitter chain separately so that at every stage there was a known-good signal path to test against. When something didn't work, the fault was contained to one module.

Approach

  • Read the schematic as a set of stages with defined inputs and outputs rather than one net.
  • Verified VFO frequency and checked transmit harmonics on a spectrum analyzer.
  • Wound toroids to spec and measured the effect of turn spacing on inductance.
  • Debugged by bisection: inject at the midpoint, determine which half fails, repeat.

Licensing

With the build nearly complete I studied for and passed the FCC Technician class exam, which I currently hold. Most of the RF theory on the exam covered material I had already had to measure directly.

Residential PV + Battery System

Energy systems MATLAB ECEN 2410 · Spring 2026 Team of 5

The system

A rooftop photovoltaic array with battery storage, sized for a residential home in Tucson, Arizona and simulated across a full year of real solar resource data from SAM. The usable roof area was 15 m² and each Qcells 300 W module covers 1.62 m², which caps the array at nine modules. We settled on eight in a 4-series × 2-parallel layout — 2.4 kW at standard test conditions, and a symmetric string configuration that keeps the array inside the inverter's window: 129.6 V at maximum power against a 100 V minimum MPPT threshold, and 158 V open circuit against a 480 V ceiling.

What I owned

This was a five-person project. My part was the electricity pricing and evaluation layer — the piece that turns a battery schedule into an actual bill. I wrote the MATLAB for the tariff structures (flat rate, current time-of-use, and a three-tier TOU scheme, each with separate summer and winter pricing) and the three cost baselines everything gets measured against:

  • No PV — hourly demand priced straight off the grid at the TOU rate.
  • PV only — generation subtracted from demand, with only the shortfall purchased.
  • PV + battery — generation and the charge/discharge schedule together, with grid import floored at zero, since this model doesn't sell back.

I also reorganized the results structure so pricing evaluations were stored per season, per tariff, and per forecast model, which is what made the comparison plots possible without rerunning the simulation for every chart.

Results

Under the current TOU tariff in summer, a home with no PV ran $39.71 a week. Adding the array brought it to $30.49. Adding the battery brought it to $14.94 — a far larger drop than the panels alone produced. The reason is timing rather than volume: the battery isn't just reducing grid consumption, it's reducing grid consumption specifically during the 4–9 pm peak when electricity is most expensive. Midday surplus that would otherwise be curtailed gets stored and spent against the highest rates of the day.

Winter followed the same pattern at a smaller magnitude — $7.27 a week with PV and battery. Interestingly, the cheapest tariff differed by season: flat rate won in summer for the no-PV and PV-only cases, while three-tier TOU was cheapest in winter. A flat rate gives a battery nothing to arbitrage against, so storage stops paying for itself.

The result I didn't expect

The team compared three forecasting models — persistence, three-day moving average, and linear regression — by mean absolute percentage error. Persistence was the most accurate in summer at 1.45% MAPE, since Tucson skies are consistent enough that yesterday predicts today. Winter wrecked all three: 81.81%, 70.76%, and 69.62% respectively.

But the most accurate forecast didn't produce the cheapest week. The moving average model had the lowest weekly cost in both seasons despite never having the best MAPE — $14.85 in summer and $6.07 in winter. Forecast error only matters where it changes a scheduling decision, and a model that's wrong in the right direction can still charge and discharge at the right hours. That gap between prediction accuracy and decision quality was the most useful thing I took from the project.
